\begin{abstract}
A web application firewall must decide to block or allow each request using only the
information that request contains. Deception is normally used later, after a session
has already been judged hostile. We use it earlier, while the decision is still open.
When the system is unsure about a client, it adds a small invisible probe to the
response. A browser never shows the probe on the page, while a client that acts on it
reveals itself. The system decides when to probe by computing what the answer is
worth, not by a threshold set by hand. Under ordinary cost accounting no middle
option exists at all, so the probe cannot be tuned into existence. In a randomised
comparison over 99 runs, probing raised the share of attackers caught by 7.0
percentage points, and no harmless session was ever diverted.

\keywords{Cyber deception \and Intrusion detection \and Web application security
\and Value of information \and Cost-sensitive learning \and Honeytokens}
\end{abstract}

% ------------------------------------------------------------ 1 introduction
\section{Introduction}

A firewall must accept or reject each request on the evidence in that request alone.
That forces a choice. Act early on weak evidence and real users get blocked. Wait for
strong evidence and the attacker has already sent several requests. Learned detectors
\cite{kruegel2003anomaly,tekerek2021novel} soften the problem but do not remove it.
They still watch, wait, and then commit.

Some attacks make it worse. A request for \texttt{/records/5} looks just like one for
\texttt{/records/6}. If that record belongs to someone else, the request is an attack,
and nothing in it says so. There is no pattern to match, so a signature firewall is
blind. A learned detector struggles too, because an honest reporting tool walks the
same ids in the same order, and nothing in the traffic separates the two.

Deception is the usual answer to that blindness
\cite{provos2004honeyd,pawlick2019taxonomy}, but most systems use it after the
decision: they judge a session hostile, then move it to a honeypot. We use it before.
While a session is still in doubt, its replies carry something extra. A browser never
shows it, but anyone reading the raw response will see it -- a fake database error
naming a table that does not exist, or an unused field in a JSON reply. We call these
additions \emph{probes}. An honest user notices nothing, while a client that acts on a
probe reveals itself.

Building a probe is engineering. Our question is \emph{when} to use one. A probe buys
no protection, because the request still reaches the application. Its only value is
the information it may reveal, and that value can be priced
\cite{howard1966information}.

\paragraph{Contributions.} This paper makes two contributions.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A priced third action.} Probing costs more than passing at every belief,
so under ordinary cost accounting there is no band for a threshold to create. The band
appears only once the information a probe buys is priced, which makes its edges follow
from the cost table rather than from a choice of ours.

\item \textbf{A measured causal effect.} Inside the probing system we withhold the
probe from one session in ten, chosen at random, so its effect is a randomised estimate
rather than a comparison of two different designs.
\end{enumerate}

% -------------------------------------------------------- 2 literature review
\section{Literature Review}

We group earlier work into five areas and state what each leaves open.

\paragraph{Detecting web attacks.} Signature firewalls match known bad patterns, so
an attack written in valid syntax slips past them entirely, and a known payload can be
rewritten until it no longer matches. Learned
detectors replace fixed patterns with a model: Kruegel and Vigna
\cite{kruegel2003anomaly} built an early anomaly detector for web requests, Robertson
et al. \cite{robertson2006generalization} generalised it across sites, and later work
applies deep \cite{tekerek2021novel} and adaptive \cite{amouei2022rat} methods. All
share one habit. They watch a session gather evidence and then commit to a decision.
Our detector belongs to this family and is not our contribution.

\paragraph{Telling scripts from people.} A separate line of work asks whether a client
is automated, not whether it is hostile. Iliou et al. \cite{iliou2021detection} show
that advanced bots reproduce browser behaviour closely enough that automation signals
alone stop separating them from people. This is why we score automation and hostility
separately. A scanner is automated and hostile, a price-comparison bot is automated and
harmless, and a careful human attacker is hostile but barely automated. A single score
cannot keep those three apart.

\paragraph{Deception and honeytokens.} Deception as a defence is well studied. Provos
\cite{provos2004honeyd} built an early virtual-honeypot framework, Almeshekah and
Spafford \cite{almeshekah2014planning} argue it must be planned around a target belief,
Pawlick et al. \cite{pawlick2019taxonomy} give a taxonomy, Beltr\'an-L\'opez et al.
\cite{beltran2025cyberdeception} survey the current state of the art, and
Ferguson-Walter et al. \cite{fergusonwalter2021examining} study whether decoys change
what attackers do. In nearly all of this work the deception is a \emph{place}: a caught
attacker is moved there, after the decision has been made. Tripwires are equally long
established -- honeyfiles \cite{yuill2004honeyfiles}, decoy documents
\cite{bowen2009baiting}, fake passwords \cite{juels2013honeywords} -- but that work
leaves them always on. Kahlhofer and Rass \cite{kahlhofer2024applayer} review deception
placed inside the application, and Kahlhofer et al. \cite{kahlhofer2025koney} automate
deploying and rotating traps, yet neither decides \emph{when} to place one from what
the system currently believes. Traps are also not free to hide: both honeypots
\cite{vetterl2018bitter} and honeytokens \cite{srinivasa2020honeytoken} have been
fingerprinted at scale.

\paragraph{Generated fake environments.} Language models increasingly produce
convincing fake systems. Sladi\'c et al. \cite{sladic2024shellm} generate shell output
live, and Bridges et al. \cite{bridges2025sok} review the area and find the gains so
far modest. Such work measures how enticing a planted item is
\cite{kahlhofer2024honeyquest}, and Vero et al. \cite{vero2026honeyval} add stealth and
fidelity. None of it asks whether a fake system contradicts itself during a long visit.
That is what we measure. Our fake site is also built so its content generator can be
swapped, which shows the result does not turn on how the content was produced.

\paragraph{Decision theory.} The mathematics we use is standard. Howard
\cite{howard1966information} defined the value of sample information, Elkan
\cite{elkan2001foundations} set out cost-sensitive decisions under a loss matrix,
Pawlick et al. \cite{pawlick2019leaky} model deception as a signalling game with
evidence, and Axelsson \cite{axelsson2000baserate} showed how the rarity of attacks
constrains any intrusion detector. None of it includes an action that buys evidence.
That is our gap. We claim no new mathematics: the contribution is noticing that a probe
\emph{is} a purchase of information, and that pricing it as one turns a hand-set option
into a calculated one.

% ------------------------------------------------------------ 3 methodology
\section{Methodology}

\subsection{System Overview}

Our system is a reverse proxy in front of the real web application. Clients only ever
talk to the proxy, so every request reaches the application through it, and the
application never knows the deception exists.

Every request follows the same five steps.

\begin{enumerate}
\item Identify the session using a cookie.
\item Turn the request into 18 numbers, called features.
\item Update two running scores for the session.
\item Price the three possible actions and take the cheapest.
\item Write the decision to a log that cannot be edited afterwards.
\end{enumerate}

The three actions are \emph{pass}, \emph{probe} and \emph{divert}. Pass sends the
request to the real application. Divert moves the session into a fake copy of the
site. Probe adds a probe to the response and passes the request on unchanged.

\subsection{Two Scores, Not One}

The 18 features split into two groups, because the threat has two parts.

Ten \emph{automation} features describe how the client behaves. They include the
time between requests, how regular that timing is, whether images and stylesheets
were fetched, how complete the browser headers are, and whether a cookie was sent.

Eight \emph{hostility} features describe what the client is trying to do. They measure
unusual characters in user input, database keywords in a suspicious position, the
share of replies that were errors, failed logins, and how many different accounts the
session tried to reach.

Two logistic models turn these features into two scores. Both scores build up across
the session. We keep them apart because the cost of an action depends only on
hostility: an automated client may be perfectly harmless. So hostility drives the
decision, and automation only chooses \emph{which} probe to plant, because a script
and a person take different bait.

\subsection{Choosing an Action by Price}

At any moment the system holds a belief $p$ that the client is hostile. Each action
carries a cost, set out as a loss matrix \cite{elkan2001foundations}. These costs were
fixed and hashed before any data was collected, so no threshold derived from them could
be adjusted afterwards to improve results. Table~\ref{tab:costs} gives them.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{The fixed cost table.}
\label{tab:costs}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
\toprule
 & pass & probe & divert \\
\midrule
client is harmless & 0  & 1  & 200 \\
client is hostile  & 25 & 25 & $-20$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Three numbers in Table~\ref{tab:costs} carry the argument. Diverting a harmless client
is the worst outcome, so it costs 200. Probing a hostile client costs the same as
passing one, both 25, because the request still reaches the real application either
way. The only extra cost of probing is 1, paid by harmless clients.

Using these costs, the expected cost of each action is a straight line in $p$:
%
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[C_{\text{pass}}] = 25p, \qquad
\mathbb{E}[C_{\text{probe}}] = 1 + 24p, \qquad
\mathbb{E}[C_{\text{divert}}] = 200 - 220p .
\label{eq:costs}
\end{equation}

Now compare the first two terms of Equation~(\ref{eq:costs}):
%
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[C_{\text{probe}}] - \mathbb{E}[C_{\text{pass}}] = 1 - p \;\geq\; 0 .
\label{eq:gap}
\end{equation}

The gap in Equation~(\ref{eq:gap}) is $1-p$, which is never negative. Probing therefore
never costs less than passing, so on cost alone the system would never probe. It would
only pass or divert, switching
where $25p = 200 - 220p$, at $p \approx 0.816$. A reader may reasonably suspect our
probing band is a threshold we chose. It cannot be, because at this stage there is no
band to choose.

\subsection{Pricing the Information}

A probe is worth something only because of what it may tell us. It has two outcomes --
the client takes the bait or it does not -- and either way we know more than before.
We measure this with the expected value of sample information \cite{howard1966information}:
%
\begin{equation}
V(p) = \min_a \mathbb{E}[C(a) \mid p] \;-\;
       \mathbb{E}_Z\!\left[ \min_a \mathbb{E}[C(a) \mid p'(Z)] \right].
\label{eq:evsi}
\end{equation}

In Equation~(\ref{eq:evsi}), $Z$ is what we observe, and $p'(Z)$ is the updated
belief after seeing it. Let $\beta_a$ be the chance a hostile client takes the
bait, and $\beta_b$ the chance a harmless one does. Then the chance of a bite is
$p\beta_a + (1-p)\beta_b$, and the updated beliefs follow from Bayes' rule. Every
term inside Equation~(\ref{eq:evsi}) is a straight line, so $V(p)$ can be worked
out directly. No simulation is needed.

The system then prices probing at its own cost minus the value of the information
it buys, and picks the cheapest action:
%
\begin{equation}
\text{price}(\text{probe}) = 1 + 24p - V(p).
\label{eq:price}
\end{equation}

Two facts fix the band at both ends. First, $V(p) \ge 0$ always. The smallest of
several straight lines is concave, so by Jensen's inequality the expected cost after
seeing the outcome cannot exceed the cost before it. Information never makes a
decision worse. Second, $V(0) = V(1) = 0$: when we are already certain, no answer
changes what we do. So the band is bounded away from both ends, and its width is a
consequence of the numbers rather than a choice.

With our fixed costs and measured bite rates, minimising
Equation~(\ref{eq:price}) gives:
%
\begin{equation}
\text{pass if } p < 0.065, \qquad
\text{probe if } 0.065 \le p < 0.879, \qquad
\text{divert if } p \ge 0.879 .
\label{eq:bands}
\end{equation}

Note what Equation~(\ref{eq:bands}) does to the divert point: offering a probe pushes
it \emph{up}, from 0.816 to 0.879. A system that can probe is more patient with a
harmless client than one that cannot. We did not aim for this. It falls out of the
arithmetic.

\subsection{Measuring the Probe, and Tools Used}
\label{sec:tools}

The calculation above needs one measured input: how often each kind of client takes
the bait. We measure it in a separate round. That round runs after the probes are
built, and its result is locked before the evaluation begins. For our main probe, 138
of 183 hostile sessions took the bait, and 0 of 74 harmless sessions did. We turn
these counts into rates using the posterior mean under a Jeffreys
Beta$(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$ prior, which gives $\beta_a = 0.753$ and
$\beta_b = 0.0067$. The smoothing matters for $\beta_b$. A hard zero would make the
likelihood ratio infinite, and it would assert a certainty that 74 sessions cannot
support.

As the conference guidelines require, we state which tools were used and where. All
detection code, features, decision rule and experiments are our own, written in
Python; no language model is used anywhere in the request path, nor for data
processing or filtering, visualisation, or proving any result. Language models were
used in exactly three places, all of which affect results reported here. First, as
\textbf{simulated attackers}: three local open models (Llama 3.2 1B, Llama 3.2 3B and
Qwen 2.5 7B) each read the replies and chose their own next request, and none was told
that probes existed. This tests our assumption about how curious an attacker is.
Second, as a \textbf{content generator} for the fake site in one comparison, to check
that our consistency layer does not depend on how the content is produced. Third, for
\textbf{adversarial testing}, attacking our own running system to look for mistakes;
it found several flaws in the fake site, which we fixed and report in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

We compare three systems. B2 and B4 are the same code and the same locked model,
selected by one configuration flag; B1 is an independent signature baseline. Two further
arms defined in the project are not reported here: an undefended proxy, and passive
scoring with a static decoy. The latter cannot differ from B2 on recall, because a
decoy changes only what a diverted session sees, never whether it is diverted.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{B1}, a signature firewall using fixed patterns.
\item \textbf{B2}, our detector without probes. It can only pass or divert.
\item \textbf{B4}, the full system, with probes and the fake site.
\end{itemize}

Each system was run over 99 separate rounds of generated traffic. Each round has
120 attack sessions and 80 harmless ones. That gives 11{,}880 attack sessions and
7{,}920 harmless sessions per system. Within one round, all three systems see exactly
the same traffic, so every attack session can be compared directly across systems.

Throughout this paper, \emph{diverted} means the system decided the client was
hostile. It is counted the same way in every system, so the numbers are comparable.
The design follows the evaluation guidance for machine learning in security
\cite{sommer2010outside,arp2022dos}: the comparison is paired and pre-registered, and
the attack rounds that train and test the detector are disjoint.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{What the Probe Itself Causes}

Comparing B2 and B4 compares two whole systems, which differ in more than the probe.
To isolate the probe we withhold it, at random, from about one session in ten among
those reaching the probing band. Both groups sit at the same belief under the same
rules; the only difference is whether a probe was sent. Table~\ref{tab:holdout} gives
the outcome.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{The randomised comparison inside the full system.}
\label{tab:holdout}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
\toprule
group & sessions & caught & rate \\
\midrule
probe sent      & 10{,}643 & 10{,}112 & 0.950 \\
probe withheld  & 1{,}237  & 1{,}089  & 0.880 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:holdout} shows a difference of \textbf{7.0 percentage points}. The
95\% confidence interval is $[5.2, 8.8]$, and Fisher's exact test gives
$p = 3.4 \times 10^{-19}$. Because the two groups were separated at random at the
same belief, the probe caused this difference.

We also checked the random split was fair. The largest compositional difference
between the groups across the five attack types is 1.2 percentage points. A chi-square
test gives 2.58 on four degrees of freedom, well below the 9.49 that would signal a
problem. An unfair split would have measured the mixture, not the probe.

\subsection{Comparison With Baselines}

Table~\ref{tab:baselines} compares the three systems.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{Attack recall and harmless sessions diverted, over 99 rounds.}
\label{tab:baselines}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llr@{}}
\toprule
system & attack recall (95\% CI) & harmless diverted \\
\midrule
B1 signature firewall & 0.366 [0.358, 0.375] & 0 / 7{,}920 \\
B2 detector only      & 0.889 [0.883, 0.894] & 4 / 7{,}920 \\
B4 full system        & \textbf{0.943 [0.939, 0.947]} & \textbf{0 / 7{,}920} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Recall and harmless diversions pull in opposite directions -- higher recall is better,
zero diversions is best -- and B4 wins on both. The recall ranges for B2
and B4 do not overlap. Comparing the same sessions in both systems, 842 attacks were
caught by B4 but missed by B2, while 197 went the other way; a paired test gives
$p = 1.9 \times 10^{-95}$, and B4 led in all 99 rounds, which rules out a lucky
result.

Those 197 are not noise, and we do not present them as such. They are delays. Their
belief landed between 0.816 and 0.879, where B2 diverts at once and B4 first asks a
question, and some sessions end before the question is answered. That is the real
cost of having a third action.

The two zeros deserve an explanation. B1's is unsurprising: its patterns need
suspicious syntax that normal traffic does not contain. B4's is more interesting,
because B4 is not merely as safe as B2 but safer. All four harmless sessions B2
wrongly diverted were probed instead in B4, and none took the bait. That silence kept
their belief below 0.879, where B2 had already acted at 0.816.

B1 is a firewall we wrote, and a reader may reasonably distrust it. So we replayed the
same traffic through the OWASP ModSecurity Core Rule Set, which many real sites use.
Table~\ref{tab:crs} gives the result at each of its four settings, two of which score
identically.

\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}

Two points follow, and one caveat. At its default setting the real rule set scores

diverting nobody. One point favours the rule set, and we state it plainly: it is judged
on blocking any single request, while our systems are judged on identifying the whole
session, which is the harder task.

\subsection{Where the Probe Helps}

The pricing makes a specific prediction: the probe should help where the detector is
genuinely unsure, and nowhere else. Table~\ref{tab:where} tests this.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\caption{Recall by attack type, over the same 99 rounds.}
\label{tab:where}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
\toprule
attack type & sessions & B2 & B4 & gain \\
\midrule
Obfuscated SQL injection & 3{,}960 & 0.898 & 0.921 & $+0.022$ \\

Password spraying        & 1{,}980 & 1.000 & 1.000 & $0.000$ \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:where} shows the whole gain sitting in one row. Walking through
records using the normal web pages improves from 0.540 to 0.823. Everywhere else
the probe changes almost nothing. This is exactly the case a signature cannot see and
the detector cannot settle, which is where the pricing says information is worth
buying.

One attack type is barely helped, and it accounts for about half of what the full
system still misses. Obfuscated SQL injection sessions do act on the bait: 18\% of
those shown a probe took it. Recall still rises only from 0.898 to 0.921, because the
detector settles most of these sessions without help. An obfuscated payload leaves
traces in the request itself, so the belief is seldom in doubt, and a probe that
confirms what the meter already knows changes no decision. The gain is small because
little uncertainty was left to resolve.

\subsection{Do People Notice the Fake Site?}
\label{sec:human}

Diverting a client is only safe if the fake site does not announce itself, and until
now that judgement was our own. A small blind study assigned each participant at
random to either the genuine application or the fake copy. They explored for about ten
minutes with an ordinary staff login. We then asked whether the system was real or a
mock-up. Both arms got the same instructions and the same questions. Nobody was told
what the study was about. Assignment is block-randomised from a fixed seed, so it
cannot be quietly re-drawn.

Four people took part, two per arm. One of the two shown the fake site called it a
mock-up; so did one of the two shown the genuine application. The difference between
the arms is zero, and Fisher's exact test gives $p = 1.0$.

That $p$ proves nothing on its own, and we would rather say so than let it flatter
us. With two participants per arm the test cannot return anything below $0.333$,
even had every participant in one arm and none in the other called the system fake.
No significant result was reachable at this size; our protocol asks for eight, the
smallest number at which a clean separation would reach $p < 0.05$. Showing that the
two arms are alike, rather than merely failing to separate them, is an equivalence
question \cite{schuirmann1987comparison} and needs a sample we do not yet have.

The study still supplies something no automated check can. A participant exploring
the \emph{genuine} application called it a mock-up because the staff directory
grouped three departments together and left a fourth apart.
People call real systems fake on the strength of an ordinary layout quirk, so the right
baseline for this question is not zero.

% ------------------------------------------------------------ 5 discussion
\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Our results support the mechanism, not just a headline number. Had the probe raised
recall everywhere, that would point to a weak detector rather than a correct
calculation. Instead the gain sits where the decision is in doubt, exactly as the
pricing predicts. The safety result has the same shape: the higher divert point was not
a feature we added, and its effect was to recover every mistake the simpler detector
made.

\paragraph{Where our approach does not win.} Setting the band edges by hand beats
calculating them, if expected cost is the only measure, and we report that rather
than hide it. Two belief values account for over half of all decisions, so the
extra precision of a calculated edge does no real work. Every setting that beats it
does so by diverting harmless clients. Among settings that divert nobody, our calculated
edges are the best available: a smaller claim than we first hoped to make, and a more
useful one.

\paragraph{Adversarial testing of the fake site.} A language model agent attacking
our own system (Section~\ref{sec:tools}) found four ways to tell the fake site from
the real one, each found by attacking the fix for the one before. A page returned
different values after a diversion. A list page named a person differently from their
own profile. A record existed after a diversion that had not existed before. The fake
dashboard claimed records as the client's own that had belonged to someone else on the
real site. A human volunteer later found a fifth: two profiles carrying the same name
but different details. All five are fixed. The lesson generalises. Checking that a
fake site agrees with itself is not the same as checking that it agrees with what the
attacker already saw. This is a different failure from the fingerprinting of deployed
decoys \cite{vetterl2018bitter,srinivasa2020honeytoken}, which reads the artefact
rather than its history.

\paragraph{Who actually takes the bait.} Bite rate does not rise steadily with the
attacker's capability, and that bounds our central claim. Across the three local
models it climbs from 0.077 to 0.556 as the model gets stronger. Outside that range,
at both ends, it is zero. Off-the-shelf tools never read the replies at all. At the
other extreme, neither the frontier-model agent nor our human tester has taken a
single bait: two interactive sessions, eighteen probes shown, none touched. Both were
still caught, by the injection path rather than by a probe. Our 7.0-point
gain is therefore corroborated only in the middle of the range. A careful reader treats
a planted database error the way we would, as something to leave alone. How
enticing a planted item looks is itself measurable \cite{kahlhofer2024honeyquest},
and calibrating our probes against a human panel is the obvious next step. This
narrows where the mechanism applies rather than withdrawing it.

\paragraph{An attacker who knows about the probe.} A reader will ask what happens once
the adversary learns to ignore probes. Offering a third action raises the divert point,
so an attacker who keeps the belief inside the band and never bites could be probed
indefinitely instead of diverted. The pricing answers this without a new parameter. A
session that has declined the same probe $n$ times has already answered the question.
Its expected information is therefore discounted by $(1-\beta_a)^n$, which is the
chance a hostile client refuses that many times in a row. As that discount falls,
probing stops being
the cheapest action and the rule converges to the two-action comparison it would have
made with no probe at all. The guarantee is asymptotic rather than per-session: a short
session sitting inside the band can still be deferred. At $n = 0$ the discount is
exactly 1, so every band derived above is unchanged.

\paragraph{Limitations.} Four, in order of importance. First, all our traffic is
generated, so every rate describes our own traffic and would need measuring again on
real traffic; the calculation and the randomised design do not depend on that, but the
numbers do. Second, we tune and test on one application: the locked detector kept
working in front of a second, very different one, but the probes themselves would need
rewriting. Third, only four people have tested the fake site
(Section~\ref{sec:human}). Fourth, the bite rate rests on an assumption about
curiosity that no human we have observed has yet borne out.

% ------------------------------------------------------------ 6 conclusion
\section{Conclusion}

Passive defences force a choice between acting too early and acting too late, and that
choice can be avoided. A defender can create evidence by placing a small invisible
probe in a reply, and when to do so can be calculated rather than guessed. Our
measurements support the mechanism: the gain appears only where the detector is
genuinely unsure, a randomised comparison shows the probe caused it, and no harmless
session was ever diverted. What remains is to test the idea against real traffic and
real attackers.
